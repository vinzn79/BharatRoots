विभाग 6

Indian Values and Life Skills

Practical values taught through stories, traditions, reflection and everyday practice. Each short course has a story, a key idea, a small action to try this week, and a question to talk about. No lectures.

How each short course works

1. Story

A short tale from India's many traditions.

2. Pause

One question to think about.

3. Idea

The value in plain words.

4. Practice

One small action for this week.

6.1 Seva: service with humility

Story: The true bargain (from the Sikh tradition). When young Nanak was a teenager, his father gave him twenty rupees and told him to make a good business deal. On the road, Nanak met a group of hungry holy men. He spent all the money on food for them and came home empty-handed. When his father asked about the deal, Nanak said he had made a sacha sauda, a "true bargain." Langar, the free community kitchen found in every gurdwara, grew from this spirit.

Pause: Was Nanak's deal a good one? Why?

Idea: Seva means selfless service: helping without wanting praise or reward. It appears across Indian traditions, from Sikh langar to temple kitchens and Sufi shrines.

Practice: Do one helpful thing at home this week without being asked and without telling anyone.
6.2 Ahimsa: nonviolence and compassion

Story: Mahavira and the angry snake (from the Jain tradition). A fierce serpent called Chandkaushik frightened everyone near its forest. Mahavira walked calmly into the forest and stood in meditation. The snake bit him, but Mahavira didn't strike back or run. He looked at the snake with calm compassion and spoke gently. The snake's anger melted, and from then on it lived peacefully.

Pause: Why do you think kindness worked when fear and force had not?

Idea: Ahimsa means not harming any living being in action, word or thought. It is central to Jainism and Buddhism, important in Hinduism, and became a tool of political change in Gandhi's non-violent resistance.

Practice: For one week, try a "no harm" challenge: no unkind words, rescue insects instead of swatting them, and pick one meal to make kinder to animals or the planet.
6.3 Gratitude

Story: Sudama's handful of rice (from the Bhagavata Purana). Sudama, a poor childhood friend of Krishna, went to visit him in his palace. He was embarrassed that his only gift was a small bundle of flattened rice (poha). Krishna greeted him with a hug, joyfully ate the poha, and treasured it more than any jewels. When Sudama returned home, he found his family's life had changed for the better.

Pause: Why did a small gift mean so much?

Idea: Gratitude means noticing what we've received and valuing the giver's heart more than the gift's price. The Tamil classic Tirukkural teaches that a kindness, even a small one done at the right time, is greater than the whole world.

Practice: At dinner, each person names one thing they are thankful for today. Keep a family gratitude jar.
6.4 Mindfulness and breath

Story: The lute string (from the Buddhist tradition). A monk named Sona was trying so hard to meditate that he exhausted himself. The Buddha asked him, "When you played the veena, did it sound good when the strings were too tight?" "No," said Sona. "And too loose?" "No." "Only when they were neither too tight nor too loose?" "Yes." "Effort is the same," said the Buddha. This is the Middle Way.

Pause: When do you push too hard? When do you give up too easily?

Idea: Mindfulness is calm, gentle attention to the present moment. Breath-awareness is found in yoga (pranayama), Buddhist meditation (anapanasati) and many other Indian practices.

Practice: The 4-4-6 breath. Breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out slowly for 6. Repeat five times before school, work or sleep.
6.5 Family relationships

Story: Bharata and the sandals (from the Ramayana). When Rama was sent into exile, his younger brother Bharata was offered the throne. Bharata refused to take what he felt was his brother's place. He walked into the forest to bring Rama back. When Rama wouldn't break his promise to their father, Bharata carried Rama's sandals (padukas) back to Ayodhya, placed them on the throne, and ruled only as a caretaker until Rama returned.

Pause: What did Bharata value more than power?

Idea: Indian traditions value respect for elders, care for younger ones, and loyalty across generations. Healthy families also need listening, fairness, and room for each person to grow. Families come in many shapes, including single-parent, blended, adoptive, chosen and multi-generational families.

Practice: Interview a grandparent or elder for 15 minutes about their childhood. Write down one thing that surprised you.
6.6 Conflict resolution

Story: Two cats and a monkey (a Panchatantra-style fable). Two cats found a piece of bread and quarrelled over sharing it. A clever monkey offered to divide it "fairly." He broke it into two uneven pieces and took a bite from the bigger one to "even it out," then from the other, and so on, until nothing was left. The monkey left with a full stomach and the cats with nothing.

Pause: What could the cats have done instead?

Idea: When we can't resolve conflict ourselves, we may lose more than we hoped to win. Good conflict resolution means cooling down, listening to understand, saying how you feel without blaming, and finding a solution both sides can accept.

Practice: Use the "Pause–Listen–Share–Solve" steps in the next disagreement at home. Pause (breathe), Listen (repeat back what you heard), Share ("I feel… when…"), Solve (brainstorm one fair option together).
6.7 Ethical decision-making

Story: Yudhishthira and the dog (from the Mahabharata). At the end of their lives, the Pandava brothers and Draupadi set out on a final journey towards the Himalayas. One by one, his companions fell, until only Yudhishthira and a loyal stray dog remained. When the god Indra arrived to carry Yudhishthira to heaven, he said the dog couldn't come. Yudhishthira refused to abandon a creature that had trusted him, even if it meant giving up heaven. The dog then revealed itself as Dharma, testing him, and he passed.

Pause: Was Yudhishthira right to risk losing a reward to stay loyal?

Idea: Dharma is often understood as doing what is right in a situation. Hard choices can be tested with questions: Is it honest? Is it fair to everyone affected? Would I be comfortable if everyone knew? Does it cause unnecessary harm?

Practice: Discuss a real dilemma, for example "Should I tell a teacher a friend copied homework?", using those four questions.
6.8

Stories, not lectures

India has an enormous library of teaching stories. Children remember a story long after they forget a rule. Good sources to explore:

PanchatantraHitopadeshaJataka tales (Buddhist)Jain kathasSikh sakhisSufi talesRamayana & MahabharataTirukkuralKabir's dohasTenali RamaAkbar–BirbalFolk & tribal tales

Story format we use

  1. Tell the story in 2–3 minutes.
  2. Ask one open question. Let children answer before adults do.
  3. Name the value in simple words.
  4. Choose one small, real action for the week.
  5. Check in a few days later: how did it go?

A doha by Kabir (15th century)

बुरा जो देखन मैं चला, बुरा न मिलिया कोय।
जो दिल खोजा आपना, मुझसा बुरा न कोय॥

Meaning: I went looking for bad people, but found none. When I searched my own heart, I found no one worse than me. Look inward before judging others.
