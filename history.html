Lessons

2.1 India Before History: Landscapes and Early Societies

To understand India's history, start with its land. The Himalayas form a high wall to the north and feed great rivers with snow-melt. Below them, the Indo-Gangetic plains are some of the most fertile land on Earth. The Deccan plateau fills the south-centre, framed by the Western and Eastern Ghats. Long coastlines connected India to Africa, Arabia and Southeast Asia.

Rivers and monsoons decided where people settled. Rivers gave water, fertile silt and transport routes. The seasonal monsoon decided when to plant and harvest, and that rhythm still shapes many festivals today.

Early humans lived in the subcontinent for a very long time. The painted rock shelters of Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, preserve thousands of years of rock art. Farming villages such as Mehrgarh (in today's Pakistan) show people growing wheat and barley and keeping cattle from around 7000 BCE.

How do we know?

  • Excavations uncover buildings, streets and burials.
  • Artifacts such as pottery, tools, beads and seals show daily life and trade.
  • Inscriptions on rock, pillars and copper plates record decisions of rulers.
  • Coins reveal rulers' names, dates, trade and even religious symbols.
  • Texts such as religious works, poems, travellers' accounts and court histories add voices, each with its own viewpoint.
Activity: Draw a simple map of India's big landforms (mountains, plains, plateau, desert, coasts). Mark three rivers and guess where early villages might have grown. Then check your guesses on the map page.
2.2 Indus Valley Civilization

Around 4,500 years ago, one of the world's earliest urban civilizations flourished along the Indus river and its neighbours, at the same time as ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia. Archaeologists call it the Indus Valley or Harappan Civilization, after Harappa, the first city found.

Key sites

  • Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are now in Pakistan. Mohenjo-daro's "Great Bath" is one of its best-known structures.
  • Dholavira (Gujarat) has remarkable water reservoirs. It was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021.
  • Rakhigarhi (Haryana) is one of the largest known Harappan sites.
  • Lothal (Gujarat) has a large basin that many archaeologists interpret as a dockyard.

What made these cities special

Streets were laid out in a grid. Houses were built of standard-sized baked bricks, and covered drains ran along the streets, a level of public sanitation rare in the ancient world. Weights were standardised across a huge area. Seals, beads and pottery travelled far, and Indus goods have been found in Mesopotamia.

What we still don't know

The Indus script has not been deciphered. We don't know for certain what language people spoke, how they were governed, or exactly what they believed. Claims that confidently link the Indus people to one later community or religion go beyond the evidence. Careful historians say "we don't yet know" where the evidence is silent.

Activity: Build a model Indus city from clay, cardboard or LEGO. Include a grid of streets, covered drains, a granary-like storehouse and a dockyard. Can you design a drain that carries water away from every house?

Sources: Archaeological Survey of India · UNESCO: Dholavira

2.3 Vedic Age and Early Kingdoms

After the Indus cities declined, a new period began, known from its texts, the Vedas. The Rigveda, a collection of hymns in early Sanskrit, is among the oldest surviving texts in any Indo-European language. It was passed down orally with extraordinary precision for centuries before it was written. UNESCO recognises the tradition of Vedic chanting as intangible cultural heritage.

Later Vedic literature, including the Brahmanas and the Upanishads, explored ritual and deep philosophical questions about the self and reality. Over time, tribal groups settled into territories called janapadas. By the 6th century BCE, sixteen larger mahajanapadas had formed, such as Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa and Avanti. Towns grew, coins appeared and trade expanded.

This was also an age of debate. Teachers questioned ritual and asked how to live well. Mahavira taught Jainism's path of non-violence and self-discipline. Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, taught the Middle Way. Other schools argued for materialism, fate or devotion.

Key idea: Ancient India was religiously and philosophically diverse. It was never a single, unchanging tradition.

By the 4th century BCE, Magadha (in today's Bihar) had become the strongest kingdom. That set the stage for the Mauryas.

2.4 Maurya Empire

Around 322 BCE, Chandragupta Maurya took power in Magadha and built an empire that eventually stretched across most of the subcontinent. Tradition links him with the strategist Kautilya (also called Chanakya). The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft, economics, law and spying, is associated with him, though scholars debate its date and authorship.

His grandson Ashoka (reigned c. 268–232 BCE) is one of history's most remarkable rulers. After the bloody conquest of Kalinga (in today's Odisha), his own inscriptions express deep remorse. He turned towards Buddhist ethics and a policy he called dhamma: kindness to living beings, respect for parents and teachers, and tolerance among religious groups.

What the Mauryas left behind

  • Edicts carved on rocks and polished stone pillars across India, mostly in Prakrit using the Brahmi script
  • Stupas, such as the core of the Great Stupa at Sanchi
  • The Lion Capital from Sarnath, now the basis of India's national emblem
  • An organised administration with provinces, officials, taxes, roads, and public works like wells and rest-houses

Primary source: from Ashoka's Rock Edict XII (paraphrased)

The king honours people of all faiths. Real honour means growing in the essentials of one's own faith while restraining speech: not praising one's own faith or criticising another's without reason. Whoever honours another's faith also strengthens their own.

This is our plain-English paraphrase of the edict's main idea. Read scholarly translations through the Indian Culture Portal and museum collections.

Map activity: Look up where Ashoka's edicts were found, for example Girnar (Gujarat), Dhauli (Odisha), Kalsi (Uttarakhand) and Maski (Karnataka). Mark them on a map. What does their spread tell you about the size of the empire?
2.5 Gupta Era

After the Mauryas, the subcontinent was shared by many powers: Shungas, Satavahanas, Indo-Greeks, Shakas and the Kushanas, whose trade links reached Rome and China. From around 320 CE, the Gupta dynasty, starting in Magadha, built a large kingdom across north India. Rulers such as Samudragupta and Chandragupta II are famous, and regional kingdoms like the Vakatakas were closely allied with them.

The Gupta period is often called a "classical age" for its achievements:

  • Literature: Kalidasa's play Abhijnanashakuntalam and poem Meghaduta are masterpieces of Sanskrit writing.
  • Mathematics and astronomy: Aryabhata (born 476 CE) wrote that the Earth rotates on its axis and calculated π closely. Indian mathematicians developed the decimal place-value system, which later travelled through the Arab world to Europe. Zero as a full number was formalised by Brahmagupta in the 7th century.
  • Art and architecture: Early stone temples such as at Deogarh, and graceful Buddhist sculpture from Sarnath. The famous Ajanta paintings were mostly made under the Vakatakas, and Ellora's caves came a little later.
  • Learning and trade: Nalanda began to grow as a great centre of learning. Ships and caravans linked India with Southeast Asia, China and the Mediterranean.
A balanced view: A "golden age" for courts and scholars did not mean an equally good life for everyone. Experiences differed greatly by region, gender, caste and class.
Activity: Make a "Gupta-era innovation card." Pick one topic (zero and decimals, astronomy, Kalidasa's writing, or temple art) and explain on one card what it is, why it mattered, and where we see it today.
2.6 Cholas and Southern India

Indian history is not only the story of the north. South India had powerful, sophisticated kingdoms with their own languages, literatures and political systems.

Sangam literature, early Tamil poetry from roughly the first centuries BCE and CE, describes love, war, kings, trade and landscapes in rich detail. The early Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas appear in these poems.

The Imperial Cholas rose from the 9th century CE. Rajaraja I built the magnificent Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur (completed around 1010), now part of the UNESCO site "Great Living Chola Temples." His son Rajendra I sent a naval expedition to the Srivijaya kingdom in Southeast Asia and founded a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram.

  • Local self-government: Inscriptions at Uttaramerur describe village assemblies chosen through a lottery (kudavolai) system.
  • Temple economy: Great temples were also banks, employers, schools and centres of music and dance.
  • Chola bronzes: Made with the lost-wax method, including the celebrated Nataraja (Shiva as the cosmic dancer).
  • Bhakti: Tamil devotional poet-saints, the Alvars and Nayanars, inspired devotional movements that spread across India.

Other southern powers

The Pallavas built the shore temples of Mahabalipuram. The Pandyas ruled from Madurai, and the Cheras ruled along the Kerala coast. The Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas ruled the Deccan; the Rashtrakutas created Ellora's rock-cut Kailasa temple. The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646) built Hampi, one of the largest cities of its time.

Activity: Using a map, mark Thanjavur, Madurai, Kanchipuram, Mahabalipuram, Hampi and the old port of Nagapattinam. Draw arrows to show sea trade routes to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and China.
2.7 Delhi Sultanate and Mughal Empire

The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) was a series of five dynasties (Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid and Lodi) that ruled from Delhi. They built the Qutb Minar, ran a system of land revenue and markets, and defended north India against Mongol invasions. Persian became a language of administration, while new forms of music, architecture and poetry developed through exchange. Amir Khusrau is a famous example.

The Mughal Empire began when Babur won the First Battle of Panipat in 1526. Key rulers:

Akbar (1556–1605) · Expanded the empire, built Fatehpur Sikri, set up the mansabdari system, encouraged debate between faiths, and abolished the jizya tax for a period
Jahangir (1605–1627) · Patron of painting and nature studies; Empress Nur Jahan held great influence
Shah Jahan (1628–1658) · Built the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort in Delhi and the Jama Masjid
Aurangzeb (1658–1707) · Largest territorial extent; long Deccan wars; policies that historians still debate
After 1707 · Rapid decline as regional powers grew; the last emperor was exiled by the British after 1857
  • Indo-Islamic architecture includes domes, arches, minarets, charbagh gardens, forts and stepwells.
  • Languages met: Persian, Arabic and Turkic mixed with Sanskrit, Hindavi and regional languages. Urdu and Hindi share deep roots from this period.
  • Culture: Mughal miniature painting, Hindustani music at court, Mughlai cuisine, fine textiles and carpets.
  • Resistance and regional powers: Rajput kingdoms, the Ahoms in Assam, the Sikh Gurus and later Sikh misls, the Marathas and southern kingdoms all shaped events.
Why careful language matters: This period included conflict, cooperation, conquest and cultural exchange. Reducing it to a simple religious contest hides how people actually lived, traded, served in armies and made art together.
2.8 Marathas and Regional Powers

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj (1630–1680) built an independent Maratha state in the western Deccan. He used hill forts, fast cavalry and a navy along the Konkan coast. He was crowned at Raigad in 1674. His administration had a council of eight ministers (the Ashta Pradhan) and promoted the use of Marathi in government.

In the 18th century, the Peshwas (chief ministers) based in Pune led a Maratha confederacy that included families such as the Scindias, Holkars, Gaekwads and Bhonsles. Maratha power reached Delhi, but the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) against Ahmad Shah Abdali was a major setback. The Anglo-Maratha Wars ended Maratha independence in 1818.

India before British rule was politically diverse

  • Sikhs: Maharaja Ranjit Singh built a strong kingdom in Punjab, with its capital at Lahore.
  • Rajputs: Kingdoms such as Mewar, Marwar and Amber.
  • Nizams of Hyderabad and the Nawabs of Awadh, Bengal and Arcot.
  • Mysore: Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan fought four wars with the British.
  • Travancore, the Ahoms of Assam and many smaller states.
Activity: Map the major 18th-century powers. For each one, note its capital, main language, and one cultural legacy, such as a fort, cuisine, art form or festival.
2.9 Colonial Rule and the Freedom Movement

The East India Company came to trade, but after the battles of Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764) it won the right to collect revenue in Bengal. Over the next century it moved from trade to territorial control through wars, alliances and annexations.

In 1857, a mutiny by sepoys at Meerut grew into a widespread uprising across north and central India, with leaders such as Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, Tatya Tope, Begum Hazrat Mahal and Bahadur Shah Zafar. After it was suppressed, the British Crown took direct control in 1858.

Colonial rule: the impact

Railways, telegraphs, universities and a modern legal system arrived, built mainly to serve imperial needs. At the same time, heavy land taxes, the decline of traditional industries such as handloom textiles, and severe famines (including the Bengal Famine of 1943) caused great suffering. Economic historians still debate the scale of "drain" of wealth from India.

Many movements, many people

  • Indian National Congress (founded 1885) grew from petitions into a mass movement.
  • Social reformers such as Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule, and Sree Narayana Guru challenged injustice.
  • Gandhi's mass campaigns included Non-Cooperation (1920–22), the Salt March (1930) and Quit India (1942).
  • Revolutionaries such as Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad and Ram Prasad Bismil.
  • Subhas Chandra Bose led the Indian National Army.
  • B. R. Ambedkar fought caste discrimination and chaired the Constitution's drafting committee.
  • Leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Sarojini Naidu and Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan.
  • The All-India Muslim League (founded 1906) increasingly argued for a separate homeland.
  • Women, workers, farmers, students, tribal and peasant movements, and countless local struggles.

Partition

Independence on 15 August 1947 came with the Partition of British India into India and Pakistan. Millions of people were displaced, and hundreds of thousands to perhaps two million died in communal violence. Many families in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh still carry these memories. Partition's causes are debated, including colonial policy, political negotiations and communal tensions, and it deserves to be discussed with care and compassion.

The Constitution

India's Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949. It came into force on 26 January 1950, now celebrated as Republic Day.

Primary source: opening of the Preamble

"WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC…"

Read the full text at the Legislative Department, Government of India.

Editorial approach: Independence was won by many people, ideas and methods, sometimes in agreement and sometimes in sharp disagreement. No single leader or community was solely responsible.
2.10 Modern India

Since independence, India has developed as the world's largest constitutional democracy, holding regular elections with hundreds of millions of voters.

1947–49 · Integration of princely states, led by Sardar Patel and V. P. Menon
1950 · Constitution in force; fundamental rights guaranteed
1951–52 · First general election, with universal adult suffrage
1956 · States reorganised largely on linguistic lines
Late 1960s · Green Revolution boosts food production
1975–77 · The Emergency; democracy restored through elections
1991 · Economic liberalisation reforms
2008, 2014 · Chandrayaan-1 to the Moon; Mangalyaan reaches Mars orbit
2016 · Unified Payments Interface (UPI) launched
2023 · Chandrayaan-3 lands near the Moon's south pole; India becomes the most populous country

India's culture travels the world through cinema, music, food, yoga, cricket, tech and startups, and a global diaspora of tens of millions of people.

Continuing challenges

Like every country, India faces ongoing questions. They include inequality and poverty, environmental pressure on air, water and forests, gender equality, religious and social harmony, and uneven regional development. Indians debate these openly. Learning about them is part of understanding India honestly.

"History is not a list of kings and battles. It is the story of how people built cities, made art, traded across oceans, argued about ideas, resisted injustice, and created the India we know today."

2.11 Caste in India: Origins, Colonial Change and the Struggle for Equality
Why this lesson matters: Caste has shaped Indian society for a very long time, and it still affects many people's lives. This lesson explains where it came from, how it changed, and how Indians have fought caste discrimination. It does not rank any community or judge anyone by birth. Discrimination by caste is against India's Constitution and the law.

Two different ideas: varna and jati

Varna is an ancient idea of society divided into four broad groups: Brahmins (priests and teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers and warriors), Vaishyas (farmers, herders and merchants) and Shudras (labourers and service-providers). It first appears in the Purusha Sukta, a late hymn of the Rig Veda. In the Bhagavad Gita (4.13), Krishna says the four varnas were made according to people's qualities (guna) and actions (karma). Many reformers read this to mean that varna was about aptitude and duty, not birth. Later law books such as the Manusmriti (written roughly 2,000 years ago) describe varna as hereditary and unequal.

Jati is what most people mean by "caste" in daily life. There are thousands of jatis, often linked to a traditional occupation and a region, with rules about whom one may marry (usually within the jati) and, in the past, with whom one could eat. Below the four varnas, some communities were treated as "untouchable" and excluded from temples, wells, schools and villages. Today many of them call themselves Dalit ("broken" or "oppressed"), a name chosen with pride. The law calls them Scheduled Castes.

Hinduism has no single, unified rulebook on caste. Its texts and traditions contain both caste hierarchy and strong criticism of it.

How old is caste?

Caste existed for many centuries before British rule. The evidence includes:

  • Texts: the Vedas, the Dharmashastras and Buddhist and Jain writings discuss varna and birth-based status.
  • Travellers: the Chinese monk Faxian (around 400 CE) wrote that people called Chandalas lived apart and had to strike a piece of wood to warn others they were coming. The scholar Al-Biruni (around 1030 CE) described the four varnas and groups outside them.
  • Genetics: a 2013 study of 73 South Asian groups found that groups in India mixed widely for about two thousand years, and then, from about 1,900 years ago, most began marrying only within their own community. That pattern of "endogamy" is a key feature of jati.

Caste was never completely frozen, though. Over the centuries, many communities rose in status by taking up new work, land or power, or by adopting the customs of higher-ranked groups (the sociologist M. N. Srinivas called this Sanskritisation). Ruling dynasties came from many backgrounds, and caste worked differently from region to region.

Not only among Hindus

Similar birth-based hierarchies developed among South Asian Muslims (for example, the distinction between ashraf and Pasmanda communities), Christians (Dalit Christians often faced separate seating or cemeteries) and Sikhs, even though the Sikh Gurus rejected caste and the langar was designed so that everyone eats together as equals.

Voices against caste, long before modern times

The Buddha and Mahavira taught that a person's worth comes from conduct, not birth. Bhakti poet-saints such as Basavanna (12th century, Karnataka), Chokhamela, Kabir and Ravidas challenged caste pride, and Guru Nanak taught the equality of all people.

What changed under British rule?

The British did not invent caste, but colonial rule changed it in important ways. In many places it made caste more rigid, more official and more political:

  • Counting and ranking in the census. From the 1870s to 1931, every census asked people their caste. Local identities that had been flexible and overlapping had to be written down as one fixed label. For the 1901 census, Commissioner Herbert Risley tried to rank castes by "social precedence" and promoted a racial theory of caste, based partly on measuring noses, which is now completely discredited. Many caste associations formed to petition the census for a higher rank or a new name, which made caste a stronger basis for organising.
  • Law based on texts. From 1772, under Warren Hastings, British courts applied Hindu and Islamic religious law to family matters. Judges relied on Brahmin pandits and Sanskrit law books such as the Manusmriti, so written, high-caste rules began to replace the great variety of local customs.
  • Branding whole communities. The Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 declared entire communities, many of them nomadic, to be "criminals by birth". Their members could be watched, restricted and confined to camps. Independent India repealed the law, and on 31 August 1952 these communities were "denotified". Many still mark the day as Vimukti Divas (Liberation Day).
  • "Martial races". After 1857 the army recruited mainly from groups it labelled "martial races", which tied opportunity to community labels.
  • New openings and new politics. Western education, new jobs, printing and cities also gave reformers new tools. The Government of India Act 1935 introduced the legal term "Scheduled Castes", so that these communities could be given special protection and representation.
A historians' debate: Some scholars, such as Bernard Cohn and Nicholas Dirks (Castes of Mind, 2001), argue that colonial rule turned caste into the single defining feature of Indian society. Others, such as Susan Bayly and Dipankar Gupta, stress caste's long history before the British and the role Indians themselves played in shaping it. Most agree on the middle ground: caste is ancient, but its modern form was strongly shaped by colonial rule.

The struggle for equality

  • Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule opened schools for girls and for children of oppressed castes, and founded the Satyashodhak Samaj in 1873.
  • Sree Narayana Guru in Kerala taught "one caste, one religion, one God for humankind". The Vaikom Satyagraha (1924–25) demanded that everyone be allowed to use the roads around a temple.
  • E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar) led the Self-Respect Movement in Tamil Nadu.
  • Dr B. R. Ambedkar, who grew up facing untouchability, led the Mahad Satyagraha (1927) for the right to drink from a public water tank, wrote Annihilation of Caste (1936), and chaired the committee that drafted the Constitution. In 1956 he became a Buddhist, together with hundreds of thousands of followers.
  • Mahatma Gandhi campaigned against untouchability. He and Ambedkar disagreed sharply, for example over separate electorates for the "Depressed Classes", which led to the Poona Pact (1932) creating reserved seats instead. Many Dalits today reject Gandhi's word "Harijan".

Caste in independent India

  • The Constitution (1950) bans discrimination based on caste (Article 15) and abolishes untouchability (Article 17).
  • Reservations (affirmative action) set aside seats in legislatures, government jobs and public universities for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and later for Other Backward Classes after the Mandal Commission (announced in 1990 and upheld by the Supreme Court in 1992).
  • Laws such as the Protection of Civil Rights Act (1955) and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act (1989) punish caste-based discrimination and violence.
  • Census 2027 is the first since 1931 to record the caste of everyone, not only Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Cities, education, new kinds of work and marriages across caste lines have weakened many old rules, and Dalit and backward-caste communities have produced leaders in every field. Discrimination has not disappeared, though, especially in marriage, housing and some villages, and Indians debate openly how best to end it. Supporters of reservations say they repair deep and continuing disadvantage. Critics argue for more emphasis on economic need or worry that benefits reach only some families.

For families: Talk about how it feels to be judged for something you did not choose. Read about one reformer above together. Agree on a family rule: no caste-based jokes, nicknames or questions such as "what is your caste?" to friends. Respect the names communities choose for themselves.
